## Facts

1. **RFC 4231** is an IETF-stream Standards Track RFC of December 2005. Its notice reads 'Copyright (C) The Internet Society (2005)', subject to BCP 78, with the authors retaining all rights not set out there. Documents published before the TLP took effect stay under the copyright policy in force at publication (RFCs 2026, 3978 and 4748 and earlier provisions), and rights not expressly granted there can be obtained only from the authors (TLP 5.0, Sections 2.c and 2.d). The Revised BSD licence that TLP Section 4 offers for code components therefore does not reach RFC 4231's test cases.
2. **RFC 8785** is an Informational RFC of June 2020 in the Independent Submission stream. Its notice names the IETF Trust and the authors and refers to BCP 78 and the TLP in effect at publication. The TLP applies to Independent Submission documents published after 28 December 2009, but its Section 4 (the Revised BSD licence for code components) does not apply to them (Section 8.f). What applies is Section 3.a without its limitation to the IETF Standards Process (Section 8.c.iii): a non-exclusive, royalty-free licence to copy, publish, display and distribute the document in whole or in part, to translate it and, unless a notice disallows it, to modify it. RFC 8785 carries no notice disallowing modification. Section 3.a names its licensees as participants in the IETF Standards Process, and Section 8 removes the references to that process without restating the licensees, so how far the grant reaches people outside the IETF is not stated in so many words.
3. **NIST examples of SHA-256** (FIPS 180-2, Appendix B, and the examples NIST publishes). Works of NIST employees are not subject to copyright in the United States under 17 U.S.C. § 105, but may be protected in other countries, and NIST asks that distributions carry a statement to that effect.

## Bearing on M0 and Q10

The README of each folder already says that the cases are MIT-licensed apart from the RFC 4231 keys and messages, the RFC 8785 examples and the NIST messages, and leaves the terms open. On the terms read here, none of the three sources is under the MIT licence or the BSD code-component licence. RFC 8785's examples come under TLP Section 3.a, which allows copying in whole or in part and modification; RFC 4231's come under the pre-2008 policy, with other rights held by the author; NIST's are public domain in the United States only. So the three directories cannot be declared wholly MIT, and REUSE-style per-file annotations that name each source and its terms (for example in a REUSE.toml or .license files beside rfc4231.json, rfc8785.json and sha256.json) would record the position correctly. Whether short test vectors such as keys, messages, numbers and digests attract copyright at all is a legal question this reading does not settle.

**Settled on 22 November 2026.** The project treats the vectors as facts, reproduced with attribution for interoperability testing, and keeps the three directories under the MIT licence without separate licence files for them. The values are digests, which the algorithms compute, and short inputs chosen to exercise the algorithms, such as repeated bytes, 'Hi There' and 'abc'. Widely used test suites under permissive licences reproduce the same values: CPython's test_hmac.py and test_hashlib.py (PSF licence) and Go's crypto/hmac tests (BSD-3-Clause) carry the RFC 4231 cases and the NIST messages (read on GitHub on 22 November 2026), and the rfc8785 Python package (Apache-2.0) carries the numbers of RFC 8785's appendix B. The README of each directory names its sources.
