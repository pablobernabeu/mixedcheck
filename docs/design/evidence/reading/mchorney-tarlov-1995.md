# McHorney and Tarlov (1995)

**Reference as checked.** McHorney, C. A., & Tarlov, A. R. (1995). Individual-patient monitoring in clinical practice: Are available health status surveys adequate? *Quality of Life Research, 4*(4), 293–307. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01593882

**Match.** Crossref (queried 22 November 2026) and the article's first page agree on the two authors, title, journal, volume 4, issue 4, pages 293–307 and year; published August 1995. Not retracted or corrected.

**Platform and pages read.** Springer Nature Link offers the article only as a PDF. The maintainer saved it, and all fifteen pages were read on 22 November 2026 outside the repository: the abstract and introduction (pp. 293–294), the measurement standards and methods (pp. 294–295), the results with Tables 1 to 5 (pp. 296–299), the discussion (pp. 299–301) and the appendix tables (pp. 305–307).

## Claims checked

1. spec/registry/checks/outcome.boundary_shares.yaml, thresholds floor and ceiling (value 0.15, descriptive): more than 15% of respondents at the lowest (highest) possible score is a common criterion for a floor (ceiling) effect.
   **Confirmed, as the origin of the criterion.** The authors propose six measurement standards for using health surveys with individual patients, one of which asks for minimal floor and ceiling effects, defined as the percentage of the sample at the worst and best possible scores (p. 294). Surveys without floor or ceiling effects exceed the standard, effects of 1% to 15% meet it, and moderate effects above 15% fail it (p. 295). Table 5 lists the standard as below 15% for both (p. 299). The authors propose the standard without deriving it from data, and they set no minimum sample size, so the registry rightly labels the threshold descriptive. Terwee et al. (2007) take the criterion from this paper (terwee-2007.md).
