# Decision record

This record resolves the ten questions raised at the end of 01-design-review.md. The decisions rest on an evidence workflow summarised in evidence/decisions/README.md. Six research reports each answered a group of questions with documentation, source code, literature and their own experiments. An adversarial verifier then re-ran the experiments and checked the sources of each report, and a consistency critic reviewed the decisions together. Where a verifier corrected a report, the corrected version is adopted here. Items that need the local session (paywalled papers, current CRAN versions and HPC runs) are listed in 03-execution-plan.md.

Three status values are used. 'Decided' means the evidence settles the question. 'Provisional' means a named check could still change the decision. 'Maintainer' means the choice belongs to the maintainer, with a recommended default.

## Q1. Python engine strategy

Python Stream A is engine-agnostic. The suite owns the canonical intermediate representation (IR) of the model, a fitted-model contract and a sparse reference evaluator of the Gaussian ML and REML objectives with weights and offsets, implemented in both languages. Engines only supply estimates, through adapters. Version 1.0 ships three things:

1. A statsmodels MixedLM adapter for fits that users already have, within what MixedLM can represent: one grouping factor with correlated slopes, nested variance components, crossed intercepts through variance components with `use_sparse=True` and a Gaussian offset absorbed into the response. The optimiser used is recorded.
2. A bring-your-own-estimates protocol, a JSON file in the contract schema, for any other engine.
3. At most one experimental adapter for a Rust-based engine, glmm or mixedlm. The choice is made at implementation time by a rule declared now: fewest contract fields the suite must reconstruct, no break in the engine's Python API over its last three releases, then label fidelity and wheel coverage.

An optional subprocess adapter can fit through the suite's own R package with Rscript, which gives Python users who have R exact lme4 parity without rpy2. There is no dependency on pymer4, juliacall or gpboost. Cross-language parity tests in CI use JSON fixtures generated by Rscript. Laplace and adaptive Gauss–Hermite evaluation and the GLMM adapters arrive with version 1.1.

A benchmark of 27 models compared each engine with lme4 1.1-35.1 and glmmTMB 1.1.8 under one engine-independent objective, which reproduced the lme4 and glmmTMB criteria to within 4e-10. The verifier reimplemented the objective independently, with agreement within 4.4e-11. They also confirmed with 200 random starts that the best value was the global optimum on every small design tested.

glmm 0.8.0 and mixedlm 1.3.0 reached the best objective on all 27 models. Both are under a year old and appear to have a single maintainer each. Both changed behaviour or public names between minor releases, although glmm's changelog states that its Python and R fit objects kept their fields.

statsmodels 0.15.0 could represent 19 of the 27 models, lacking crossed correlated slopes, weights and likelihood-based GLMMs. Its crossed variance-component fits were about 25 times slower than lme4 even with sparse matrices (23.6 s against 0.86 s at 12,000 rows). With its default optimiser it stopped 0.013 to 1.16 deviance units short of the optimum on all four near-singular designs, reporting convergence on two of them. Nelder–Mead reached the optimum on three of the four, and lbfgs reported convergence at gaps of 0.06 to 0.22, so its convergence flag cannot be trusted.

Of the other options, pymer4 cannot be installed with pip by design, and rpy2 is tied to specific R versions. gpboost's default likelihood is approximate, 0.08 to 0.69 deviance units from exact.

Every adapter supplies the following fields:

- engine, engine version, adapter version and contract version
- the IR hash and the analysis-sample row indices
- a map from engine coefficient names to canonical IR columns, with aliasing flags
- the criterion (REML, ML, Laplace, AGQ with its number of nodes, nAGQ = 0, variational Bayes or MAP) and whether the reported likelihood is exact
- the semantics of the weights (precision, frequency or log-likelihood multiplier)
- the fixed effects
- the absolute covariance matrix of each random-effects block, in canonical order with its packing order declared
- the residual variance or dispersion, in the registry's parameterisation
- the reported objective, with its constant convention
- the covariance matrix of the fixed effects, with a method label
- the raw convergence output

Optional capabilities (X and Z, labelled predicted random effects, an objective function, refit, simulate and df) are flagged, and a missing one yields 'not_available'.

The suite then evaluates its own objective at the reported estimates and compares it with the reported value. Tolerances depend on the criterion and the engine version and are stored in the registry, for example max(1e-6·|dev|, 1e-6) for LMMs. Known engine conventions, such as the Laplace deviance of lme4 before 2.1-0, are registered as offsets. A failed comparison yields a status (contract_mismatch, objective_unavailable or objective_offset_registered) rather than a crash. Stream A stops only above the scale of the semantic errors the benchmark found, the smallest of which was 0.08 deviance units.

The suite also measures the gap to the best known objective and recomputes the predicted random effects, residuals and P-based quantities itself. It reports two conditional variances separately, one with the fixed effects held fixed and one including their uncertainty, because the two differed by up to 24% on sleepstudy.

Each contract field answers a discrepancy seen in the benchmark. glmmTMB treats Gaussian weights as log-likelihood multipliers unless they are mapped through its dispersion formula. The mapping is version-dependent: −log w before version 1.1.9 and −0.5 log w from 1.1.9. Standard-error conventions differ by up to 2.4% between engines. mixedlm loses factor labels and drops a different aliased column from lme4. Reading glmm's covariance in the wrong order shifted the REML criterion by 2.3 units, yet still produced a positive-definite matrix.

Parity tiers are assigned from a conformance corpus, never in advance, and recorded in the registry's list of expected disagreements. Each assignment is specific to an engine, version range, optimiser setting, adapter mapping and output class. The tolerance tier requires the common objective within max(1e-4, 1e-9·|dev|) of the best known value and fixed effects within 0.01 standard errors. Standard errors are compared only under the same convention, or after the suite has recomputed them.

The current assignments rest on one replicate per design and on superseded engine versions. lme4 2.1-0 changed the Laplace deviance and GLMM standard errors, and glmmTMB 1.1.9 moved the Gaussian dispersion to the standard-deviation scale. The assignments are therefore provisional until they are regenerated on ARC with current versions and replicates.

A Rust engine becomes 'supported', and the documented default for new Python fits, only when three conditions hold. It must pass the conformance corpus at the tolerance tier on three consecutive releases. The fields its adapter reads must not have broken for 12 months. It must also have a second active maintainer or an institutional home. If no engine qualifies, bring-your-own estimates and the R route remain the supported paths for GLMMs in Python.

The benchmark seeds the conformance corpus. Simulated fixtures are committed, and real datasets are fetched from R at test time. glmm already validates itself against a large grid of reference fits, so the software paper presents the diagnostic contract and the design audits as its contribution and the conformance results as supporting evidence.

## Q2. Model scope

From the first 0.x release, Stream B audits cover every outcome type in both languages with exact parity. The types are Gaussian, binary, binomial with trials, counts with exposure, bounded scores, ordinal items and positive durations.

T0 checks come from declarations: type, event level, trials, exposure and bounds. T1 checks read the outcome only marginally or by cluster: constant clusters, minority events per cluster and the shares of boundary and zero values. The separation check for binary outcomes is a linear program on the fixed-effects matrix after deletion of incomplete rows. Because it relates the outcome to the predictors, it is a T2 check and is deferred in outcome-blind mode. Its output is a verdict with per-coefficient bounds over the optimal face, because different solver settings returned different optimal vertices for the same problem.

Version 1.0 of Stream A diagnoses Gaussian LMMs only. In R these are lme4 objects (lmerMod, lmerModLmerTest and afex mixed objects). In Python they are statsmodels MixedLM fits and bring-your-own estimates. Gaussian glmmTMB fits arrive with the glmmTMB adapter in version 1.1.

When a single Likert item is about to be modelled as metric, Stream B warns at T0 (Liddell & Kruschke, 2018) and states that Stream A cannot yet diagnose cumulative-link models. Stream B is released to CRAN and PyPI as a 0.x version as soon as it is complete, so that feedback arrives before Stream A is finished.

The roadmap after 1.0 runs as follows. Version 1.1 adds Bernoulli, binomial-with-trials and Poisson GLMMs. These come through glmer and glmmTMB in R, and in Python only through an engine promoted under Q1 or through bring-your-own estimates.

Version 1.2 adds negative binomial (NB1 and NB2), Gamma, beta, beta-binomial and ordered beta families, and zero-inflated and hurdle models. R comes first, and Python follows through a promoted engine, since glmm, mixedlm and gpboost already fit several of these.

Version 1.3 adds structured covariance terms with within-unit semivariograms. These are lme4 2.x `diag()`, `cs()` and `ar1()` and glmmTMB's ar1, ou, toep and cs.

Ordinal cumulative-link mixed models and an nlme adapter are candidates for version 2, started on demand. Bayesian fits, GAMMs, multiple-membership models, nonlinear mixed models and multilevel SEM are not planned for 1.x, and every report states where coverage ends.

A model class is promoted only after its checks have false-flag and detection rates validated on the HPC. Releases are gated by maintainer time rather than by compute.

GLMM diagnostics in version 1.0 would have no Python counterpart, because statsmodels has no frequentist GLMM. GLMMs also cost more to fit and to triage. In a 1,600-row crossed design with maximal random effects, the median Bernoulli glmer fit took 1.94 s, about 11 times as long as lmer.

Triage of singular and non-converged fits is core version 1.0 work even for Gaussian models. Over 100 correctly specified datasets, lmer warned in 14% of fits, Bernoulli glmer in 59% and Bernoulli glmmTMB in 67%. With the residual standard deviation set to the logistic latent value (π/√3), lmer warned in 52% of fits, so most of the difference reflects the information in the data rather than the family.

One decision rule is fixed now. If the maintainer's own accuracy data need binary GLMM diagnostics before version 1.1, an R-only module for Bernoulli and binomial glmer comes forward. It covers reporting and convergence triage, with residual checks available only as an opt-in DHARMa cross-check, and Python reports 'not_available'.

The published reviews of LMM and GLMM usage cited in the research report could be read only as abstracts. They are listed for the local session, and no prevalence claim enters the paper until they have been read.

## Q3. Stream B output and outcome-blind operation

Stream B never returns a single recommended formula. It returns a plan object with five parts:

1. the design-implied ceiling for each grouping factor, with the number of informative groups behind each term and the level-specific df budget
2. an identifiability verdict for the ceiling and for every rung, exact for Gaussian LMMs whose covariance parameters enter linearly and only a necessary condition for GLMMs
3. a deterministic ladder of nested rungs built without the outcome, with an explicit protected set
4. a stopping rule chosen from a registry menu, with a declared terminal alternative
5. a hash of all of the above, together with the coding and centring constants, the role declarations and the engine settings

The confirmatory model is defined as the result of applying rule R to ladder L, and the user, not the package, declares it primary. The plan and the report both state that the operating characteristics of the rule are known only for the simulated conditions until HPC study H1 has run.

Building the ladder starts by freezing the coding of factors, as numeric contrast columns, and the centring constants, recording the sample they came from. This matters because zero-correlation structures change with the coding. In one of the review's simulations, a zero-correlation fit gave different REML criteria (1773.66 against 1774.99), slope standard errors and df under 0/1 and ±0.5 coding. Rung 0 is the ceiling restricted to identifiable terms.

The protected set is never removed by the ladder. It contains:

- the random intercepts of replication and randomisation units
- the by-unit slopes of focal within-unit effects
- for a focal interaction, the slope of the highest-order combination of within-unit factors it contains (Barr et al., 2013)
- the lower-level slope of any focal cross-level interaction (Heisig & Schaeffer, 2019)

Unprotected terms are reduced in a fixed order. Correlations are simplified before variances are removed, nuisance terms go before anything protected, and higher-order terms go before their marginal terms. For a factor slope with three or more levels, the steps are an unstructured matrix, then compound symmetry on the cell indicators, `cs(0 + f | g)`, then a homogeneous form or complex random intercepts. Complex random intercepts equal homogeneous compound symmetry only when the correlation is non-negative, and the engines that support each step are recorded.

The order among nuisance terms is a hypothesis for study H2, because omitting correlated nuisance slopes can cost precision (Heisig et al., 2017). An empty protected set is flagged, since declaring every effect a nuisance produces an aggressive ladder.

The default stopping rule is a localised, protected back-off. The ladder steps down only in two cases. The first is a fit that fails to converge, judged by the suite's own scaled gradient and Hessian, with a second optimiser confirming the objective. The second is a boundary direction that lies in unprotected parameters, and then only those zero-variance directions are removed. Boundary directions confined to protected terms are accepted and named.

If the protected rung itself fails to converge, the declared terminal alternative applies and is labelled as an alternative estimator. Examples are a penalised covariance estimate, or cluster-robust CR2 inference for nested designs (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2018).

The registered alternatives are protected likelihood-ratio selection at α = .2 (Matuschek et al., 2017), protected AIC, no reduction (the ceiling) and an unprotected back-off, which the report flags.

The evidence supports the protected set more firmly than any particular rule. Dropping a focal slope produced Type I error rates of .226 and .187, against .054 and .035 for a nuisance slope. An intercepts-only model reached .200 in a 24-subject by 16-item design and .348 in a 48 by 32 design.

A rule that stepped down whenever any block was rank-deficient removed non-zero nuisance slopes in 40 to 45% of datasets when a protected slope had zero variance, at a cost of up to 2.6 power points. That is why the default is localised.

Localised back-off, protected LRT(.2) and protected AIC differed by at most .005 in all 14 conditions of the verifier's simulation. The choice among them is therefore a judgement, revisited after study H1 with a decision rule that is based on a metamodel with multiplicity control and measured relative to the ceiling. A naive rule such as 'switch if any cell exceeds .06' would trigger in 63% of 50-cell grids even for a rule whose true rate is .05.

Stream B runs outcome-blind (T0) by default, because the ceiling, verdict, ladder and plan need no outcome. T1 checks, which read the outcome marginally or by cluster, unlock only through an explicit outcome-access argument together with the hash of a frozen plan. Without a plan they run in a mode labelled exploratory, and a plan created afterwards is stamped as planned after outcome inspection. T2 checks, which relate the outcome to predictors or to a fitted model, run only in Stream A or in an explicit exploratory mode.

A plain JSON log records the plan hash, each tier accessed and the reason given, and the report header shows the highest tier accessed before the plan was frozen. A hash chain would add nothing that the person controlling the files cannot rewrite. Credibility comes instead from depositing the plan with a third party such as OSF or Zenodo, in the spirit of blind analysis (MacCoun & Perlmutter, 2015). For secondary data, the user attests to their prior knowledge of the outcome.

Masked execution selects and freezes the rung before revealing any estimates. It works for Gaussian LMMs because their REML and profiled ML likelihoods depend on the outcome only through residuals orthogonal to X. Refitting with a shifted focal effect left the singularity and warning flags unchanged in 799 of 800 fits. Masking is deferred to a later release as a two-step API, and GLMMs are excluded until study H9.

Release 1 contains the ladder generator, per-rung identifiability, the localised default rule, protected LRT(.2), the no-reduction rule, the plan hash with generated preregistration text and the plain log. Bayesian and penalised terminal alternatives are documented but not implemented.

A protected set as such is not new. buildmer's `include` argument and lme4's guidance on singular fits already point that way, and the lme4 documentation states that there is not yet consensus on how to choose a random-effects specification. The novelty lies in the outcome-free ladder, the plan contract and their validation.

## Q4. Design declarations

Declarations are optional by default and required by mode. Checks whose inputs the data determine run every time: label incidence (nested, crossed or partially crossed), within-unit and between-unit status, cluster sizes, rank and connectivity. Two further properties are inferred with tolerance and reported with 'inferred' wording: the level at which each predictor varies, and candidate keys among ID-typed columns.

Other properties cannot be read from the data and come only from a declaration. These are:

- whether repeated labels denote the same unit (ID scope)
- whether and at what level assignment was randomised, and the blocking
- the roles of variables and their timing relative to assignment
- the focal estimands, dyad roles and the EEG reference

Without a declaration, such a check reports 'not assessable' or 'ambiguous', names the field to declare and never recodes. For an undeclared ID scope, the report adds outcome-free evidence, such as whether unit attributes stay consistent across outer units. In mlmRev's Exam data, 269 of 322 shared student labels change sex across schools, whereas in a crossed psycholinguistic dataset none of 600 items changes its frequency or length.

A minimal declaration becomes mandatory for a confirmatory goal, for outcome-blind and preregistration exports and for trial features (cluster-randomised, stepped-wedge and partially nested designs). A `draft_design()` function in both languages writes a declaration pre-filled from inference and marks the inferred entries. The draft counts as a declaration only after the user accepts it, and in outcome-blind mode it reads only ID-typed and right-hand-side columns. A code-first builder in both languages writes the same JSON.

Every design tool reviewed takes structure as a declared input rather than inferring it, including DeclareDesign (Blair et al., 2019) and simr (Green & MacLeod, 2016). Reused labels and genuine crossing produce identical incidence patterns.

The data can show whether a treatment varies within or between units. A rule requiring 99% agreement within clusters recovered the school level in 99.3 to 100% of 2,000 replicates with up to 0.5% of rows miscoded. The data cannot show whether assignment was randomised, what the blocks were or which unit was assigned when small clusters are aliased.

Getting the ID scope wrong matters. In a 20-school design with reused class labels and class-level treatment, coding the classes as crossed gave Type I error rates of 13 to 34% in the conditions where the likelihood would have favoured the true nested structure. Using the outcome to choose between nested and crossed coding is nonetheless excluded on the outcome-blind principle.

Each dataset has one canonical JSON declaration. JSON Schema (draft 2020-12) is the normative specification. Each package ships a hand-written validator, tested against a shared corpus of valid and invalid files with expected error codes, and the jsonschema library serves as the oracle in CI. Third-party validators disagree: fastjsonschema ignored `dependentRequired`.

The table block is a subset of the Frictionless Table Schema v2: field types, categories and their order, missing values including labelled sentinel codes, and primary and unique keys. Three caveats are documented: the default `fieldsMatch` is 'exact', an absent type means 'any', and R's frictionless package lacks categories.

Readers in both languages apply the same strict rules:

- UTF-8 without a byte-order mark, with NFC normalisation
- rejection of duplicate keys, nulls, NaN and infinities
- integers limited to ±2^53 and decimals to 15 significant digits
- rejection of keys outside the Basic Multilingual Plane and of lone surrogates
- the Frictionless lexical defaults, applied explicitly

Three hashes are defined. The declaration hash is SHA-256 of the RFC 8785 canonical form. The data hash is SHA-256 of the raw file bytes. A parsed-table hash serves only the parity tests. YAML is accepted for authoring only: it is read with every scalar as a string and then converted to JSON.

These rules are needed. An R and Python prototype gave identical hashes for six example declarations and ten fixtures, but without the rules it diverged on 5 of 8 new declaration edge cases and on 4 of 9 table edge cases.

Vocabulary 0.1 is a core of eight blocks:

- table
- units, with `nested_in`, `id_scope` and membership
- crossings
- observation: the unit of one row, its key and its time
- assignment: column, unit, level within or between units, randomisation and blocks
- variables: role, `derived_from`, bounds and measurement level
- estimands: term, kind, focal flag and outcome
- provenance: creation date, author, an outcome-seen attestation and the data hash

Unknown keys are errors. A semantic layer checks references, cycles and columns, and any 0.1.x version is accepted. Dyad, EEG, imputation and time-zone detail follow in 0.2 or later. The draft schema, with examples and invalid files, is in evidence/decisions/E/schema.

## Q5. Reports, privacy and accessibility

The canonical output is the results JSON, which is always written. The default human-readable output is one self-contained HTML file built from a logic-less Mustache template shared by both languages. Formatting is done beforehand in printf style, so that the rendered strings can be parity-tested. The file contains no scripts, makes no external requests and has a print stylesheet, so that a browser can save it as a tagged PDF.

An optional export writes an engine-free Quarto Markdown folder with no code chunks. Values come from a generated metadata file. Figures are SVG files with alternative text, plus PNG copies for output checking in trusted research environments, whose checkers block SVG. The results JSON travels with its SHA-256.

Users render the export with their own Quarto to HTML, Word or PDF through Typst. Word and Typst output require Quarto 1.11 or later, because earlier versions dropped figure alternative text in Word and failed on some images with alternative text in Typst. With an older Quarto, the package warns and falls back to HTML.

The package never calls LaTeX and never depends on Quarto. In R, quarto (≥ 1.5.0) sits in Suggests behind `requireNamespace()` and `quarto_available()`. In Python, the command-line tool is located with `shutil.which()`.

Both packages also produce a preregistration block, which combines Stream B outputs with the declarations the user must supply. They also generate methods text and APA-style tables, only from the results JSON, with mandatory elements and banned phrases such as 'assumptions were met'. JARS-Quant (Appelbaum et al., 2018) sets the minimum content.

Version 1 has no interactive plots, because in the review's experiment an interactive HTML plot embedded every exact value and identifier.

Figures are static and aggregated. In the default mode they are drawn as inline SVG from the shared view model, using a few fixed layouts (bands, bars and matrices), so the core report needs no plotting package. Cross-language figure parity is tested on parsed coordinates within a tolerance. Hash comparisons are reserved for fixtures with shared draws, because aggregates computed separately in each language can differ in the last digit. ggplot2 and matplotlib versions of the figures are optional `plot()` methods.

The default privacy mode is 'shareable'. It shows no row-level values, identifiers, minima, maxima, outliers or point plots, and it applies these rules:

- every displayed cell, bin or band contains at least k = 10 distinct persons (the declared disclosure unit, not rows)
- the two largest contributors supply less than 90% of a bin's rows
- a quantile p is shown only if n·min(p, 1 − p) ≥ k
- every count below k is shown as '<10', including outlier counts and differences between the raw and analysis samples
- every cell is rounded to base 5 with the margins recomputed, or secondary suppression applies
- flagged clusters get labels that exist only in the report

Reports contain no paths, user names or host names. A banner states that shareable mode reduces disclosure risk without making the report anonymous data.

An 'internal' mode needs an explicit argument. It carries a banner and a file suffix, and it draws point-level figures as PNG. All parameters live in the registry and are recorded in the JSON.

The defaults match the confirmed defaults of the ACRO output-checking tool: a threshold of 10, a dominance rule and rounding. Pseudonymised data remain personal data under the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. The thresholds stay provisional until they have been checked against the primary UK guidance, the advice of Oxford's data protection officer and the rules of data providers.

In the experiment behind the scatter-plot rule, an SVG yielded all 300 points, and a 100-dpi PNG yielded the five most extreme residuals to within 0.04 to 0.26 units. Rasterising therefore offers no protection.

The HTML report targets WCAG 2.2 level AA (World Wide Web Consortium, 2024). The language and title are set, headings form a hierarchy, and tables have header cells and captions. Every figure has short alternative text, a long description generated from the results and its aggregated data table. Status is conveyed by text and shape, never by colour alone, and content reflows at 320 CSS pixels.

Essential marks use five colours with at least 3:1 contrast against white: blue #0072B2, vermillion #D55E00, bluish green #009E73, reddish purple #CC79A7 and black. Against each other they reach only 1.12 to 1.69, so adjacent areas are separated by white gaps or outlines and labelled directly. Orange, sky blue, yellow and grey are kept for outlined fills that carry no essential information.

Monorepo CI runs axe-core and an HTML validator on golden reports, and checks a Typst PDF/UA-1 test render with veraPDF.

## Q6. Dependencies and interoperation

All diagnostic computation is independent of easystats and DHARMa. No check takes its status, threshold or severity from performance, insight or DHARMa.

Cross-checks with those packages are opt-in, through an argument whose default is none, so that installing a package never changes a report. The cross-checks appear in a separate block without flags. They record the package versions, and for DHARMa also the seed and the number of simulations. They stay out of severity grading, the log of rules fired and the parity claims, and they include no p-value tests by default. Results can be exported as a plain long data frame.

Four reasons support keeping the computation separate. First, insight 1.7.0's convergence check, which `performance::check_convergence()` wraps, returns FALSE for non-singular fits on which lme4 skipped its derivatives, so 'not assessable' would be reported as 'not converged'. Second, insight had 22 CRAN releases and performance 13 in two years, so delegated values could change without any change to the registry version. Third, Python cannot call these functions, so every delegated value would be a permanent parity exception. Fourth, insight and performance are GPL-3, so their code can be called but not copied. DHARMa's hard dependency closure also runs to 94 packages, 49 of them compiled.

lme4 and all other engines go in Suggests, and the R package is not split. Imports are limited to Matrix, jsonlite and base packages. The dependency closures compare as follows:

| Imports | Closure | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Audit core (Matrix, jsonlite) | 3 packages | |
| Core plus lme4 | 14 packages | 7 compiled and not recommended |
| Core plus glmmTMB | 65 packages | |
| Everything | 118 packages | |

A hard dependency's R floor can also move without notice. Deriv 4.3.5 began requiring R 4.5, and that requirement now reaches glmmTMB through pbkrtest and doBy.

A demonstration package passed R CMD check both with and without Suggests. It dispatched lme4, lmerTest, afex and glmmTMB objects without importing lme4.

The availability guard sits in the generic. It reads the package that defines the object's class and, if that package is missing, returns 'not_available' with the package name before dispatch. A test diagnoses a saved .rds fit with the engine absent, because S4 dispatch on an lme4 object fails before any method-level guard can run.

Splitting the package would double the CRAN submissions and NEWS files for one maintainer without reducing install weight any further. The lme4 formula grammar is parsed in-house in both languages. reformulas, which is GPL-3 and has compiled dependencies, is used only as a test oracle in Suggests. The HTML template renderer is an in-house Mustache subset, tested against the language-independent Mustache specification tests.

The Python package mirrors this structure: numpy, pandas and scipy in the core, engines and plotting as extras, and the same 'not_available' vocabulary.

## Q7. Names and repository

The working name is mixedcheck in both languages: the CRAN package, the PyPI distribution and import package, and the conda-forge packages r-mixedcheck and mixedcheck. The fallback is lmecheck. Both names are free on CRAN (current and archived, checked case-insensitively), Bioconductor, PyPI (including its similarity rule) and conda-forge, whereas mmcheck is taken on PyPI.

CRAN names are permanent, so the name should fit the eventual scope, which extends beyond linear models to GLMMs. 'lme' and 'lmm' signal linear models only, and 'lme' also evokes `nlme::lme`, which is out of scope. 'mix' suggests mixture models or MCMC mixing, and 'audit' fits Stream B but undersells the diagnostics and reports.

The choice is the maintainer's, to be made after `pak::pkg_name_check()` and an R-universe search in the local session. The GitHub repository is renamed before the first public release. No placeholder is uploaded to PyPI, because PEP 541 prohibits name squatting.

| fixtures/ | the shared goldens, with a provenance file and per-directory licence files |
| parity/ | the cross-language harness |
| sim/ | the HPC validation code and SLURM templates, never shipped |
| paper/ | the JOSS paper |
| docs/ | the design documents |

The compiled registry is vendored byte-identically into r/inst/ and python/src/mixedcheck/, and CI fails on any SHA-256 mismatch. A .gitattributes file keeps these files with LF line endings, so that the check survives Windows checkouts.

Every install path supports subdirectories: remotes and pak references, pip's `#subdirectory=` and R-universe's `subdir` field. PyPI Trusted Publishing builds from python/. The two packages are versioned independently with language-prefixed tags (r-v*, py-v*), and each records the registry version it ships. Both sit under one Zenodo concept DOI.

The large dual-language monorepos (arrow, xgboost and LightGBM) need build-time scripts only because they share C++ code, which this project does not. All the same-name precedents (marginaleffects, pins, vetiver, pointblank and duckdb) use separate repositories, but those have separate teams. For one maintainer and one shared registry, a single repository avoids synchronising the registry and fixtures across repositories.

## Q8. Domains and test corpus

Two designs drive the defaults, the message wording and the first vignettes: nested education data, including cluster-randomised and multisite trials, and crossed subjects-by-items designs with trial-level data. These two match the maintainer's fields and span nesting and crossing. Between them they exercise most Stream B checks: label scope, randomisation level, few clusters, design df, connectivity, one observation per cell and counterbalancing.

In version 1.0, the crossed vignette uses a Gaussian outcome (log response times) for Stream A, and binary accuracy appears only in Stream B audits until GLMMs arrive. Longitudinal and intensive longitudinal data, including app logs, follow in 0.2, once the time, time-zone and lag infrastructure exists. Families and dyads follow in 0.2 or 0.3, fixtures first. Single-trial EEG comes later and stays in the monorepo, because the ERP CORE data (Kappenman et al., 2021) are large and carry a ShareAlike licence.

Defaults remain design-agnostic. Domain checks and wording switch on from declared features, or from data patterns with 'inferred' wording.

The test corpus has four layers:

1. A simulated pathology zoo, MIT-licensed and bundled in both packages. Thirty fixtures are planned and ten have been prototyped, totalling 67 KB of CSV. Each has a seeded generator, a tiny stored instance, a clean matched negative, a declaration that types its CSV, a preliminary formula, and expected findings derived from the generating design rather than from the suite. Parsing-hazard and attribute-consistency fixtures join the zoo.
2. Public datasets from R packages such as lme4, nlme, mlmRev, languageR and afex. R examples use them through Suggests and CI materialises them for parity tests, but neither package redistributes them. The licences of the data themselves could not be determined, and several of the packages are GPL or AGPL. Vignettes shared across languages therefore use zoo fixtures, which also avoids the problem that Python has no route to the languageR or afex datasets through Rdatasets.
3. Openly licensed external data, CC0 or CC BY plus a CC BY-SA folder for ERP CORE derivatives. They sit in a separate corpus folder with per-dataset licence files and a manifest of licence evidence, and each is added only after a local licence check.
4. The maintainer's restricted data. They are used only for private validation on ARC, with the data owner's permission and a data protection impact assessment, and are never committed.

HPC validation grids cover only stochastic or thresholded checks. Deterministic checks are covered by unit tests.

## Q9. Validation on the HPC and papers

The maintainer approved simulation validation on the Oxford HPC and a paper, without co-maintainers. The validation programme follows the ADEMP structure (Morris et al., 2019) and is preregistered with the ADEMP-PreReg template (Siepe et al., 2024) after a pilot. It comprises these studies:

| Study | Scope |
|---|---|
| V0 | Deterministic audits, validated by known-answer fixtures in CI |
| V1 | A Gaussian null bank on which every check runs, giving false-flag rates and family-wise flag counts |
| V2 | Targeted misspecification modules, giving detection rates and the consequences for focal estimands, judged on the whole check-then-act procedure rather than on detection alone (Shamsudheen & Hennig, 2023) |
| V3 | Singularity, convergence and boundary criteria |
| V4 | Variance-component tests and the parametric bootstrap |
| V5 | The reduction ladder (studies H1, H2 and H5) |
| V6 | Calibration of outcome-blind design analysis |
| V7 | Cross-language parity |
| V8 to V10 | The GLMM counterparts, for version 1.1 |

Failures and non-convergence count as outcomes and are never dropped (Pawel et al., 2026). The study declares itself non-neutral, because the developer evaluates their own checks. It includes comparators in their default configurations: performance, DHARMa, engine-native flags and naive variants (Boulesteix et al., 2013).

Each check is defined exactly as its registry entry before any pilot result is interpreted. The verifier showed why. A pilot ran a Bartlett test across clusters and found no inflation for the small-cluster heteroscedasticity item. The comparison the item actually names, by cluster-size stratum, gave false-flag rates of .09 to .18 at a nominal .05 with raw conditional residuals and .054 to .080 with studentised ones.

A test-based check is calibrated in a cell if the upper 95% Monte Carlo limit of its false-flag rate is at most .075 at a nominal .05. This is Bradley's liberal criterion, labelled as a convention (Bradley, 1978). A descriptive flag graded consequential needs an upper limit of at most .05.

Assessability is adequate if detection reaches .80 at the smallest harmful magnitude, weak between .50 and .80 and absent below .50. Harm means coverage below .925, Type I error above .075 or relative bias above 10%, and for null estimands it means absolute bias above a registry threshold.

Thresholds are tuned on a calibration half and confirmed on a held-out half, and verdicts across cells use a metamodel or smoothing with multiplicity control. Replicate counts follow Monte Carlo SE targets of about .005 for rates near .05 (2,000 replicates) and at most .016 for detection (1,000 replicates).

Engine versions are pinned (lme4 ≥ 2.1-0), and the language, engine and version behind each threshold are stored as registry provenance. Sentinel cells are rerun at each major engine release. This matters because, since 1.1-38, lme4 skips derivative-based convergence checks for singular fits, for lmer fits with 10 or more covariance parameters, for glmer fits with 20 or more parameters and for data with 10,000 or more rows.

Phase 1 (version 1.0) needs between about 820 and 3,050 CPU-hours across the verifier's scenarios. With current lme4 settings, Satterthwaite and Kenward–Roger inference, allFit with seven optimisers and an un-nested V6, the estimate is about 860 to 1,140. Exact cluster-deletion refits for influence raise it to about 3,050. Phase 2 (version 1.1) needs about 270. Compute is therefore not the binding constraint, whereas maintainer time is.

On ARC, each study runs as a SLURM job array on the htc cluster's short partition, which has a 12-hour limit. Each task gets one core and 2 GB, and tasks are packed to 2 to 4 hours, with at most 500 tasks per array and a concurrency throttle. Each chunk uses an L'Ecuyer-CMRG stream and each replicate a substream, so any dataset can be regenerated. Chunks are idempotent, write to scratch and are copied to /data/educ-intract/educ1242/, with completion checked against the project space.

R libraries are built into an Apptainer image off-cluster with portable compiler flags. This is needed because current glmmTMB built from source requires R 4.5 through Deriv, while ARC's documented module is R 4.4.0. If ARC's QoS limits concurrency, whole-node multi-core jobs replace the single-core arrays. ARC keeps no backups, so the code and the raw replicate results are archived with a DOI.

An HPC-0 pilot comes first. It checks the R module, the array and QoS limits and the credit balance, and it re-times a subset of fits with current lme4, since the timings so far came from lme4 1.1-35.1.

The validation study becomes a Stage 1 Registered Report, submitted after the pilot to Behavior Research Methods or to Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science. The ADEMP-PreReg authors list both journals as accepting Registered Reports of simulation studies. In-principle acceptance before the main runs protects a sole maintainer against unwelcome results, for example checks that turn out not to be assessable. The fallback is a preregistered regular article in Behavior Research Methods. Psychological Methods becomes the target only if the paper is reframed as a general comparison of diagnostic procedures.

The software paper goes to JOSS, for both packages, once the repository has more than six months of public history and evidence of research use beyond the tool's own papers. JOSS scrutinises single-author projects that show no community engagement. JSS is the single-paper alternative, which would replace JOSS.

Venue facts beyond JOSS's own documentation remain to be confirmed on the publishers' sites in the local session: scope wording, Registered Report acceptance, open-access arrangements for Oxford and review times. The work is gated by milestones rather than months (03-execution-plan.md).

## Q10. Versions and licence

The R package declares `Depends: R (>= 4.2.0)`. The current release is R 4.6.2 (29 October 2026), and the tidyverse policy of supporting the current and four previous minor versions (Averick, 2019) puts the floor at 4.2. R 4.2 also made UTF-8 the native encoding on Windows, which removes a class of cross-language string-parity failures.

CI runs Ubuntu with R-devel, release, oldrel-1 and 4.2, and Windows and macOS with the release. It adds one job without Suggests (`_R_CHECK_DEPENDS_ONLY_=true`) and one with the oldest supported lme4.

lme4 2.x needs headers from Matrix 1.5-0 or later, which R 4.2.0 and 4.2.1 do not bundle. The documentation therefore states that Stream A with lme4 2.x on those patch releases needs a newer Matrix. The floor rises only when a dependency or a feature requires it, within the five-version window.

The Python package declares `requires-python = ">=3.11"`. The rule is the oldest CPython version still receiving security fixes at the first release, but no lower than the numpy and scipy floors need. Python 3.10 reached end of life on 1 October 2026, and 3.11 receives security fixes until about October 2027, after which the floor becomes 3.12.

Core dependency floors follow SPEC 0 at release time (Mueller et al., 2020), currently numpy ≥ 2.3, scipy ≥ 1.15 and pandas ≥ 2.3. The statsmodels extra requires statsmodels ≥ 0.14.2, the first release to support NumPy 2, or ≥ 0.15.0 if the package relies on formulaic-based behaviour.

CI tests the lowest direct and the latest resolutions on Python 3.11 to 3.15. It also covers both pandas 2.3 and 3.x while 2.3 remains within SPEC 0, because pandas 3 infers a new string dtype.

Both packages, and everything else in the monorepo (registry, schema and simulated fixtures), are MIT-licensed. R uses 'MIT + file LICENSE' with a two-line stub giving the year and copyright holder. Python uses the PEP 639 licence expression with the full text. The two licence files are kept separate.

Running GPL-2, GPL-3, AGPL-3 or LGPL dependencies does not conflict with an MIT package. The R FAQ states that the GPL restricts only the distribution of R or of programs containing its code. The Expat (MIT) licence is GPL-compatible, and the network clause of the AGPL-3 binds only modified versions. CRAN also distributes many permissive packages with GPL-family dependencies, 25 in a sample of 171.

Code is never copied or translated from GPL or AGPL packages. Algorithms are implemented from papers and documentation, and the provenance of each is logged. Third-party data enter fixtures only under CC0 or CC BY with attribution. Any inlined third-party JavaScript, CSS or fonts would need their own notices, which the report design avoids.
