**Match.** Crossref (queried 22 November 2026) and the publisher's page agree on the ten authors, journal, volume 24, issue 1, pages 29–40, January 1995 and DOI. **Platform and pages read.** ScienceDirect, through Oxford's subscription, which offers the article only as a PDF; the browser could not render it as text, so the maintainer saved the PDF and it was read on 22 November 2026 outside the repository: pp. 29–34 (abstract, introduction, methods with Figure 1, measures, statistical analysis and the first results with Table 1).

## Claims checked

The claims are those of flay-1988.md: E/tables/q8_datasets.csv describes mixor::SmokingPrevention (TVSFP) as 1,600 students with school and class identifiers, school-level 2 × 2 randomisation (classroom curriculum, television) and a pre-test, and reports.json (research:declarations-data) asks for the randomisation unit and the school and class counts.

1. Randomisation unit. **Confirmed.** Within each of the two counties, entire schools were assigned to conditions by the randomised multiattribute blocking of Graham et al. (pp. 30–31). The pretest showed no significant differences in smoking rates across the seven conditions (p. 31).
2. Design. **Confirmed, with an arm and a site that the CSV leaves out.** The abstract calls it a '2 × 2 plus 1' design. Figure 1 (p. 30) gives five conditions in Los Angeles, the social-resistance curriculum with television, television only, the curriculum only, an attention-control placebo and a no-treatment control, with seven schools per condition, and two in San Diego, the curriculum only and a no-treatment control, with six schools per condition; no San Diego school received the televised intervention. The authors analyse it as an incomplete three-factor design (site, classroom curriculum, television; p. 32, footnote 7). The 2 × 2 that q8_datasets.csv describes is therefore the four factorial conditions of Los Angeles, 28 schools if all were kept, without the attention control and the San Diego replication.
3. Counts. **Confirmed for the whole trial; not for mixor's subset.** The initial sample was seventh-grade students in 340 classrooms within 35 Los Angeles and 12 San Diego schools in six school districts; 7,351 students were pretested in January 1986 and 6,695 (91%) reported gender, ethnicity and smoking status (p. 30). The paper does not say which students, classes or schools make up the 1,600 students in mixor, and its tables count students per condition and wave, not classes. mixor's documentation of SmokingPrevention (version 1.0.7 on CRAN, read on 22 November 2026) describes 1,600 observations, a school variable identifying 'the Los Angeles school', a class variable and indicators of randomisation to the classroom curriculum, to the television intervention and to both, and cites Flay et al. (1988) and Hedeker and Gibbons (1996, 2006). The data therefore come from the Los Angeles part of the trial, coded as its 2 × 2 of curriculum and television. The documentation gives no counts of schools or classes, and does not say whether the attention-control schools, which this coding cannot tell apart from the no-treatment controls, are among them. Hedeker (2015; hedeker-2015.md) describes a sample of the same trial with the same size, site, design and variables: 1,600 students in 135 classrooms of the 28 Los Angeles schools randomised to the four arms of the factorial, without an attention-control arm, kept because they had complete knowledge scores at both tests. This settles the counts from published sources, without opening the data.
4. Pre-test and waves. **Confirmed.** Pretest in January 1986, immediate post-test in April 1986, and follow-ups in April 1987 and April 1988 (p. 30); 3,155 students (47%) remained at the two-year follow-up, with more attrition in Los Angeles and none differential by condition (p. 30).
5. Clustering, for the vignette. The analyses treat students within classrooms within schools as three-level data. Intraclass correlations ranged from 0 to 0.03 for schools and from 0 to 0.05 for classrooms (p. 33, footnote 9). Ordinal outcomes were analysed with random-effects ordinal models fitted at two levels, students within schools or within classrooms (p. 33), which is the route by which these data reached ordinal mixed-model software.
